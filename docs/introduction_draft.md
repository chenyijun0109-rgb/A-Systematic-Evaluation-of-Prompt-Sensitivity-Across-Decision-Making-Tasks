# Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being studied not only as language technologies but also as behavioural objects. Because they can receive natural-language instructions, interact with experimental environments, and generate repeated choices, researchers have used them to reproduce classic findings, simulate heterogeneous respondents, and explore hypotheses from psychology, behavioural science, and economics (Argyle et al., 2023; Aher et al., 2023; Horton, 2023). Cognitive tasks have likewise been used to characterise the behavioural regularities and apparent biases of successive GPT models (Binz and Schulz, 2023a; Hagendorff et al., 2023). This work creates a potentially useful methodological role for LLMs: they can serve as inexpensive, reproducible computational systems whose behaviour can be examined under controlled task manipulations. However, an LLM run is not a human participant, and behavioural resemblance does not by itself establish shared cognitive mechanisms. LLM outputs are conditional samples from systems trained on text and subsequently shaped by instruction tuning, deployment settings, and the immediate linguistic context. Their scientific value as cognitive models therefore depends not only on whether they can perform a task or resemble a human reference sample, but also on whether the behavioural conclusions drawn from them remain stable when experimentally irrelevant or theoretically secondary features of that context change (Binz and Schulz, 2023b; Shanahan et al., 2023).

Prompt formulation is central to this problem. A prompt does more than transmit task rules: it also frames the model's role, highlights particular features of the environment, and establishes expectations about how a response should be produced. Seemingly minor changes in prompt wording, formatting, demonstrations, or option order can produce substantial changes in model performance and choice (Lu et al., 2022; Loya et al., 2023; Sclar et al., 2024; Pezeshkpour and Hruschka, 2024). Role instructions are especially relevant when an LLM is asked to behave as a study participant, because such framing can elicit a particular simulated persona rather than reveal a context-independent disposition (Shanahan et al., 2023). Prompt sensitivity is thus not merely an engineering inconvenience. In behavioural research, the prompt is part of the experimental measurement procedure. If two formulations communicate the same rules and available information but yield materially different patterns of exploration, learning, or risk taking, then substantive conclusions about the model may partly reflect the instrument used to elicit them.

Existing research provides strong evidence that prompting matters, but it does not yet fully resolve the reliability question addressed here. Much LLM evaluation emphasises task accuracy, aggregate human likeness, or the replication of a single empirical pattern. Prompt-robustness studies, by contrast, have often focused on benchmark accuracy, few-shot example order, superficial formatting, or isolated decisions (Lu et al., 2022; Sclar et al., 2024; Razavi et al., 2025). Decision-making studies have shown that wording and decoding parameters can alter LLM choices (Loya et al., 2023), yet fewer studies have examined whether controlled, meaning-preserving prompt manipulations change repeated behaviour over complete interactive tasks, whether those effects differ across model variants, and whether greater prompt stability coincides with better task performance or closer agreement with human distributions. These distinctions matter. A model can be consistently non-human-like, human-like only under one formulation, or variable across prompts while maintaining similar average rewards. Stability, performance, and human similarity are therefore related but non-equivalent evaluation criteria.

Language introduces a further source of contextual variation. Multilingual LLMs do not necessarily exhibit equivalent capabilities or response distributions across languages, even when prompts are translations of the same task. Cross-lingual evaluations have documented uneven performance and imperfect transfer between English and other languages (Ahuja et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). Consequently, evidence obtained exclusively from English prompts cannot automatically be assumed to describe a model's behaviour in Chinese or Spanish. At the same time, cross-language comparisons are difficult to interpret if language, model version, task implementation, and prompt content vary simultaneously. A controlled comparison should therefore hold the model and experimental environment fixed and use semantically matched prompts, while acknowledging that translation can preserve experiment-relevant content without making expressions identical in every pragmatic respect.

The present study examines behavioural reliability in three established decision-making paradigms that capture different aspects of adaptive choice. The Horizon Task separates information seeking and horizon-related changes in exploration within an explore--exploit problem (Wilson et al., 2014). The Iowa Gambling Task (IGT) measures learning from repeated gains and losses when advantageous long-term options must be distinguished from disadvantageous ones (Bechara et al., 1994; Toplak et al., 2010). The Balloon Analogue Risk Task (BART) measures sequential risk taking through repeated decisions to increase potential reward while accepting an increasing probability of loss (Lejuez et al., 2002). Together, the tasks move beyond one-shot question answering and sample exploration, feedback-based learning, and risk management. They also provide established human datasets against which run-level LLM behaviour can be compared (Steingroever et al., 2015; Feng et al., 2021; Sebri et al., 2023). Human proximity is treated here as descriptive evidence about behavioural distributions, not as proof that an LLM implements the same latent processes as people.

Across these tasks, the study manipulates four prompt conditions while holding task rules, environment parameters, response requirements, and sampling settings constant: a neutral baseline, a more specific instruction formulation, a human-participant role framing, and a task-specific emphasis on the construct most relevant to the task. Each model--task--prompt cell is repeatedly sampled, allowing prompt effects to be estimated from distributions of complete task runs rather than from a single response. The main English comparison evaluates GPT-4.1, GPT-5.4, and GPT-5.4 Mini under the same frozen prompts and task environments. A complementary multilingual comparison holds the model fixed at GPT-4.1 and compares semantically matched English, Simplified Chinese, and Spanish prompts. The design deliberately separates model comparisons from language comparisons, so that neither factor is interpreted through a comparison in which the other also changes.

This study makes three contributions. First, it operationalises behavioural reliability as robustness to controlled prompt reformulations and measures prompt effects at the level of task-specific behaviour. Second, it tests whether prompt sensitivity varies across models, tasks, and behavioural constructs, rather than reducing reliability to a single overall ranking. Third, it separates prompt stability from task performance and human-reference proximity, while extending the analysis beyond English through a fixed-model multilingual comparison. The study addresses four research questions: **RQ1**, to what extent do instruction specificity, role framing, and task-specific construct emphasis alter behaviour relative to a neutral baseline? **RQ2**, do the direction and standardised magnitude of these prompt effects differ across models and tasks? **RQ3**, do alternative prompt formulations change substantive conclusions about models' decision tendencies or their proximity to human reference data, and what does this imply for their reliability as cognitive models? **RQ4**, when the model, task environments, prompt-manipulation meanings, and generation settings are held constant, do English, Simplified Chinese, and Spanish produce different baseline behaviours or within-language prompt effects? By answering these questions, the study evaluates not simply whether LLMs can complete cognitive tasks, but whether the behavioural claims derived from those tasks are sufficiently stable to support scientific interpretation.
